## Costophrenic angle blunting

```yaml
id: pleura-effusion-costophrenic
findings: [pleural_effusion]
confusions: [consolidation]
aspect: radiographic_sign
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pleural-effusion"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pleural effusion"
```

On an erect PA chest radiograph, free pleural fluid collects in the most
dependent part of the pleural space, which is the costophrenic recess. The
normally sharp, acute angle where the diaphragm meets the chest wall becomes
shallow and then disappears. Loss of that crisp angle is usually the earliest
sign visible on a frontal film. Roughly 200 ml of fluid is needed before the
lateral costophrenic angle blunts on a PA view; the posterior recess is deeper,
so a lateral film shows smaller volumes, from about 50 ml. A sharp costophrenic
angle on an erect film therefore argues against a large effusion, but does not
exclude a small one.

## The meniscus sign

```yaml
id: pleura-effusion-meniscus
findings: [pleural_effusion]
confusions: [consolidation, nodule_or_mass]
aspect: radiographic_sign
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pleural-effusion"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pleural effusion"
```

Free pleural fluid has a concave upper border that rises higher laterally than
medially, giving a curved meniscus rather than a flat line. This is a surface
tension effect at the fluid–lung interface, not a fluid level. A genuine
horizontal fluid level on an erect film implies gas in the pleural space as well
as fluid, which points to a hydropneumothorax, a bronchopleural fistula or
recent intervention. The meniscus is the sign that most reliably separates
pleural fluid from lung consolidation at the base: consolidation opacifies the
lung but leaves the lateral angle and the fluid contour absent.

## Distinguishing effusion from basal consolidation

```yaml
id: pleura-effusion-vs-consolidation
findings: [pleural_effusion, consolidation]
confusions: [consolidation, pleural_effusion]
aspect: differential
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pleural-effusion"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pleural effusion"
```

Both produce a dense lower zone on a frontal film, and mistaking one for the
other is among the commonest errors at this level. Consolidation tends to have
an ill-defined upper margin, often contains air bronchograms, and follows lobar
anatomy with a boundary at a fissure. An effusion has a smooth concave meniscus,
no air bronchograms, and blunts the costophrenic angle. Effusions may shift with
position; consolidation does not. The two coexist frequently in pneumonia, so
finding one does not rule out the other, and describing which features are
present is more useful than asserting a single label.

## Subpulmonic effusion

```yaml
id: pleura-effusion-subpulmonic
findings: [pleural_effusion]
confusions: [no_finding]
aspect: pitfall
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pleural-effusion"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pleural effusion"
```

Fluid can collect between the lung base and the diaphragm without spilling into
the costophrenic recess, so the costophrenic angle stays sharp and the film can
be read as normal. Clues are an apparently raised hemidiaphragm, a diaphragmatic
dome whose highest point sits more laterally than usual, and a widened gap
between the gastric air bubble and the apparent left diaphragm. It is the
classic reason a moderate effusion is missed on an erect film. A lateral
decubitus view, or ultrasound, settles it.

## Pleural fluid on a supine film

```yaml
id: pleura-effusion-supine
findings: [pleural_effusion]
confusions: [no_finding, consolidation]
aspect: pitfall
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pleural-effusion"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pleural effusion"
```

On a supine or semi-erect film, which is what most portable AP radiographs are,
fluid layers posteriorly rather than pooling at the base. The result is a
generalised veil-like increase in density over the affected hemithorax, through
which lung markings remain visible, often with a hazy apical cap. The
costophrenic angle may look normal. Because density increases gradually rather
than forming an edge, a substantial effusion can be overlooked entirely or
mistaken for underexposure. Always check the projection before concluding a
supine film shows no effusion.

## The visceral pleural line

```yaml
id: pleura-pneumothorax-line
findings: [pneumothorax]
confusions: [no_finding]
aspect: radiographic_sign
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pneumothorax"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pneumothorax"
```

A pneumothorax is diagnosed on a frontal film by identifying the visceral
pleural line: a thin, sharply defined white line running roughly parallel to the
chest wall, with lung markings visible on its inner side and absent beyond it.
The absence of vascular markings peripheral to that line is the confirming
feature, not the primary one — hypovascular lung and overexposure can both mimic
it. On an erect film, air rises, so the line is usually sought first at the apex
and along the lateral chest wall.

## The deep sulcus sign

```yaml
id: pleura-pneumothorax-deep-sulcus
findings: [pneumothorax]
confusions: [no_finding]
aspect: pitfall
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pneumothorax"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pneumothorax"
```

On a supine film, pleural air collects anteriorly and basally rather than at the
apex, so the classic apical pleural line may be absent altogether. Instead the
affected costophrenic sulcus appears abnormally deep, lucent and sharply
defined, extending further inferiorly than the opposite side. The ipsilateral
hemidiaphragm may look depressed and the adjacent heart border unusually crisp.
Because supine and portable films are exactly the circumstances in which a
pneumothorax matters most, this sign is worth looking for specifically on any AP
film.

## Skin folds and other pneumothorax mimics

```yaml
id: pleura-pneumothorax-mimics
findings: [pneumothorax]
confusions: [pneumothorax]
aspect: pitfall
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pneumothorax"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pneumothorax"
```

A skin fold produces an edge that can be mistaken for a pleural line, but it is
a broad band with a gradual density transition rather than a fine white line,
it often extends beyond the chest wall, and lung markings can usually be traced
across it. Clothing, tubing, bedding and the scapular edge do the same. The
decisive test is whether vascular markings genuinely stop at the line: if
vessels cross it, it is not a pneumothorax. Calling a skin fold a pneumothorax
risks an unnecessary chest drain, so the threshold for this diagnosis on a
portable film should be the presence of both features, not one.

## Tension pneumothorax

```yaml
id: pleura-pneumothorax-tension
findings: [pneumothorax]
confusions: []
aspect: management
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/tension-pneumothorax"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Tension pneumothorax"
```

Tension physiology is suggested radiographically by mediastinal shift away from
the affected side, depression or inversion of the ipsilateral hemidiaphragm, and
splaying of the ribs. It is a clinical diagnosis and treatment should not wait
for a film. The imaging point for a student is that a large pneumothorax with
contralateral mediastinal shift is an emergency finding rather than a
descriptive one, and should be reported as such immediately.
