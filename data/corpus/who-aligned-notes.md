## External artefacts that mimic disease

```yaml
id: who-external-artefact-mimics
findings: [nodule_or_mass, no_finding]
confusions: [nodule_or_mass]
aspect: pitfall
```

Not every opacity is anatomy. Clothing, jewellery, ECG electrodes and their wires, and braided hair all project over the lung fields and can imitate nodules, lines or even a pneumothorax edge. The clues are external: unnaturally straight edges or perfect circles, a density that continues beyond the chest wall onto the soft tissues or the background, and symmetry that follows clothing rather than anatomy. Before reporting a solitary opacity, check whether it respects anatomical boundaries. Removing the object and re-imaging settles the doubt faster than any further interpretation.

## Empyema versus a simple effusion

```yaml
id: who-empyema-vs-simple-effusion
findings: [pleural_effusion]
confusions: [consolidation]
aspect: differential
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/empyema-vs-pleural-effusion-2"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Empyema vs pleural effusion"
```

An empyema is infected fluid occupying the pleural space, and on a frontal film it looks much like any other effusion: dependent opacity, meniscus, blunted angle. Features favouring empyema include loculation with a lentiform (lens-shaped) collection, visible pleural thickening, and adjacent consolidation from underlying pneumonia. The split-pleura sign is a CT finding, not a plain-film one. Because the film alone rarely separates them, the correct student report describes the fluid and its distribution, and notes that clinical context — fever, raised inflammatory markers, recent pneumonia — determines the next step, not the radiograph.

## Haemothorax after trauma

```yaml
id: who-haemothorax-after-trauma
findings: [pleural_effusion]
confusions: [no_finding]
aspect: differential
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hemothorax-1"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Haemothorax"
```

Blood in the pleural space behaves radiographically like any pleural fluid: a meniscus on the erect film or a veil of generalised density on the supine film. What points to haemothorax rather than a medical effusion is context, not appearance — recent trauma, fractured ribs, a chest drain already in situ, or associated contusion. A rapidly enlarging opacity after injury suggests ongoing bleeding and is urgent. Describe the fluid accurately, pair it with the history, and state plainly when the film alone cannot distinguish blood from other fluid.

## Interstitial oedema in left heart failure

```yaml
id: who-lvf-interstitial-oedema
findings: [cardiomegaly, consolidation]
confusions: [consolidation]
aspect: radiographic_sign
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pulmonary-oedema"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pulmonary oedema"
```

In left heart failure the lungs show the backlog before the heart looks dramatically enlarged. Early signs are interstitial: fine septal (Kerley B) lines at the bases, peribronchial cuffing, and upper-lobe vascular prominence. As oedema worsens it becomes perihilar "bat-wing" consolidation that characteristically spares the lung periphery — the reverse of most pneumonias. An enlarged cardiac silhouette with small bilateral effusions alongside these lung changes strongly favours oedema over infection. Describe the distribution first; it is what separates cardiac from infective consolidation.

## Upper-lobe blood diversion

```yaml
id: who-lvf-upper-lobe-diversion
findings: [cardiomegaly]
confusions: [no_finding]
aspect: radiographic_sign
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pulmonary-oedema"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Pulmonary oedema"
```

On a normal erect film the lower-lobe vessels are more prominent than the upper-lobe vessels because of gravity. In early left heart failure this gradient reverses: the upper-lobe vessels become as conspicuous as the lower ones, termed cephalisation or blood diversion. It precedes interstitial oedema and frank consolidation, so it is the earliest plain-film clue to raised left atrial pressure. The sign is only valid on an erect inspiratory film — supine positioning redistributes blood to the apices physiologically and produces the same appearance in a normal patient.

## The solitary pulmonary nodule: what the plain film owes

```yaml
id: who-solitary-nodule-workup
findings: [nodule_or_mass]
confusions: [consolidation]
aspect: differential
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/solitary-pulmonary-nodule-1"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Solitary pulmonary nodule"
```

A solitary pulmonary nodule on CXR is a finding, not a diagnosis. The plain-film report should record size (nodule up to 30 mm, mass above), location, margins and any visible calcification, then stop: characterisation belongs to CT. The single most useful step is comparison with old films — stability over two years strongly favours benignity, while growth demands workup. Multiple nodules shift thinking toward metastases, granulomas or vascular causes. A student who detects the lesion, describes it completely and states what comparison is needed has done the whole plain-film job.

## Lobar pneumonia and the fissure boundary

```yaml
id: who-lobar-pneumonia-fissure
findings: [consolidation]
confusions: [pleural_effusion]
aspect: radiographic_sign
read_more_url: "https://radiopaedia.org/articles/consolidation"
read_more_label: "Radiopaedia: Consolidation"
```

Bacterial lobar pneumonia fills an entire lobe with inflammatory exudate, producing homogeneous opacity bounded sharply where the lobe meets a fissure — a straight edge that effusions and most masses do not show. Air bronchograms within the opacity confirm an alveolar process, and crucially there is no volume loss: the fissures stay in place and the mediastinum does not shift. The silhouette sign then names the lobe. A parapneumonic effusion often accompanies lobar pneumonia, so look for a meniscus alongside the consolidation rather than forcing a single label.
